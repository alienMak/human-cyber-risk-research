## What isn't covered yet

The first matrix batch omitted studies from 2016, 2018, 2019, 2022 and 2023; the additional screening table below now supplies examples from those years. Coverage is still not exhaustive. Existing separate commentary notes partly cover 2019, but annual screening is incomplete. Papers about physical social engineering, voice/deepfake attacks, children, cross-vector simulations, cybersecurity culture, and fairness in behavioral risk scoring also require dedicated searching. Do not assign inclusion/exclusion counts until an auditable screening process is performed.

## Cross-study interpretation checked 15 October 2026

Nicholson, Coventry & Briggs (SOUPS 2017, [publisher record](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2017/technical-sessions/presentation/nicholson), pp. 285–298) report that sender-salience cues improved phishing discrimination in their task, while confidence remained poorly calibrated for phishing emails. **Status updated 31 October 2026: FULL TEXT APPRAISED**, §§3–5 and Tables 1–6; [detailed note](../literature-watch/2026-10-31-sender-cue-salience-and-confidence-calibration.md). The printed discrimination p-values appear consistent with one-sided testing, and one reported degrees-of-freedom value needs clarification; the result is not evidence of real-world reporting or durable behavioral change.

Hasegawa et al. (SOUPS 2021, [paper](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/soups2021-hasegawa.pdf), §§3–5) surveyed 862 non-native English speakers in Germany, South Korea and Japan and report that lower English confidence was associated with ignoring English-language messages without careful inspection. **Review status: existing 14 October full-text working appraisal, with publisher abstract rechecked on 15 October; no new independent reappraisal claimed.**

**Synthesis, not an authors' combined finding:** better detection with a visible cue and unverified avoidance of a difficult-to-process message are different outcomes. Neither paper alone tests whether highlighting sender details improves verification behavior across languages. Do not pool these studies as if their designs or endpoints matched. The [15 October literature-watch note](../literature-watch/2026-10-15-language-and-verification.md) sets out a possible future test.


## Decision accuracy, response bias and external validity (23 October 2026)

The following are **distinct canonical publications**, screened against original journal records and cross-checked against existing notes. They are not counted as three completed full-text appraisals: two full texts were reviewed and one historical paper was checked at abstract level. The existing Canfield **2017 replication** is a separate publication and is not duplicated here.

| Year | Study | Venue and source | Design and measured outcomes | Main finding and boundary | Review status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2016 | Canfield, Fischhoff & Davis — [Quantifying Phishing Susceptibility for Detection and Behavior Decisions](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720816665025) | *Human Factors* 58(8), 1158–1172 | Two online scenario-based experiments; detection judgments versus stated behavioral decisions; manipulated task order/base-rate information | Publisher abstract distinguishes limited discrimination from cautious stated responses; real inbox behavior not established by this abstract | **ABSTRACT-ONLY** for this run; full-text sample and estimates not extracted |
| 2024 | Sturman et al. — [The roles of phishing knowledge, cue utilization, and decision styles in phishing email detection](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2024.104309) | *Applied Ergonomics* 119, 104309 | Cross-sectional online classification task, 145 Australian adults; 15 phishing and 15 genuine emails; sensitivity, bias, hits and false alarms | Cue-utilization grouping associated with discrimination; knowledge associated with both more phishing hits and more benign-message misclassification. **No reporting or causal training outcome** | **FULL TEXT APPRAISED**: §§5–7, Tables 5–9; [detailed note](../literature-watch/2026-10-23-classification-errors-and-reasonable-reporting.md) |
| 2025 issue (online 2024-10-27) | Morrison et al. — [Like Shooting Phish in a Barrel](https://doi.org/10.1177/15553434241296170) | *Journal of Cognitive Engineering and Decision Making* 19(1) | Quasi-experimental trait-group comparison; 94 Australian university students; 40-item controlled task and two analyzed inbox simulations | Cognitive reflection associated with fewer controlled-task false positives; cue utilization/reflection did **not** significantly predict engagement with either limited inbox simulation. Null results are not proof of no association | **FULL TEXT APPRAISED**: *Method; Results; Discussion > Limitations*; [detailed note](../literature-watch/2026-10-23-classification-errors-and-reasonable-reporting.md) |

**Cross-study boundary:** In both full-text studies, “false positive” means incorrectly **classifying** a genuine email as phishing. It does not mean that an employee inappropriately **reported** a legitimate email. A report's reasonableness at decision time was not directly measured. Do not pool the studies' estimates or use controlled classification as validated individual-level workplace prediction.

## Longitudinal reporting and repeated simulation behavior (8 November 2026)

| Year | Study | Design and operational outcomes | Finding and inference boundary | Review status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2021 | Canham, Posey, Strickland & Constantino — [*Phishing for Long Tails*](https://doi.org/10.1177/2158244021990656), *SAGE Open* 11(1) | Observational analysis of 20 simulated email campaigns over 19 months at one US university; 6,126 clustered employees; recorded clicks, replies, data entry (available in only 3 campaigns), reports; any unsafe action counted as one campaign failure | >3 failures: 6% of employees, 29% of failures; high-reporting group: 33% of employees, 92% of reports (rounded shares, Tables 1–2). **Interpretive boundary:** The published “risk factor” is a ratio of observed shares, not a validated prospective individual risk estimate. Do not pool with training trials or infer cross-vector transfer.
