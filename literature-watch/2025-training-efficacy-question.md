# What should we conclude when phishing training has little measurable effect?

**Source:** [Anti-Phishing Training (Still) Does Not Work: A Large-Scale Reproduction of Phishing Training Inefficacy Grounded in the NIST Phish Scale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.19899)  
**Authors:** Andrew T. Rozema and James C. Davis  
**Published:** June 2025 (preprint)  
**Source type:** Research preprint  
**Added:** October 2026

## The finding

The authors report an operational study with 12,511 participants at a US financial technology company. They found no statistically significant main effect of the evaluated training interventions on click or reporting rates. The difficulty of phishing lures, assessed with the NIST Phish Scale, was associated with observed click differences: the preprint reports 7% clicking for easy lures and 15% for difficult ones.

## Why this matters for human cyber risk

The result challenges the assumption that completing training automatically translates into changed behavior. It also complicates comparisons across campaigns. A higher click rate can reflect a more difficult lure rather than worse awareness.

For simulation-based research, this strengthens the case for tracking context and challenge difficulty alongside the outcome. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that any alternative simulation method works better.

## Where the argument needs care

This is a preprint and describes one organizational environment and particular interventions. A non-significant effect is not universal proof that all forms of training are ineffective. Claims about VISHWAR would need their own comparative design and evidence.

## Question to pursue

Could a future study compare static instruction, scenario-based simulation and a combined intervention using matched difficulty and delayed follow-up, rather than judging effectiveness from immediate click rates alone?
