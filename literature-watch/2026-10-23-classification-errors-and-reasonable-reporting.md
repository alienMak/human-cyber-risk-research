## Sturman et al. (2024): classification versus discrimination

**Design and population:** Cross-sectional, observational online study of **145 Australian adults** (64 men, 81 women; ages 22–86), recruited through an age-stratified Qualtrics panel. Recruitment involved participant self-selection; age stratification does not make this a nationally representative probability sample. No training intervention was assigned and there was no randomized treatment comparison. See §5.1, pp. 2–3, and §5.3, p. 5.

**Task and operational definitions:** Participants categorized **30 emails** (15 genuine, 15 phishing) into ten categories, including separate “Spam” and “Phishing” labels. They were told in advance that **half** the messages were phishing. Emails appeared for ten seconds before categorization; simulated phishing items were constructed with multiple suspicious features. The authors also measured objective phishing-feature knowledge, email security awareness, decision style and cue utilization using separate tasks. “Hit” meant a phishing email categorized as phishing; “false alarm” meant a genuine email categorized as phishing. These are **classification outcomes**, not real-world reports to an IT team. See §§5.2.1–5.3, pp. 3–5, and §6.2, p. 5.

**Analysis and results:** The paper separates discrimination (sensitivity) from general response tendency (criterion). Participants were grouped into relatively higher (n=75) and lower (n=70) cue-utilization clusters; multivariable linear regressions then examined sensitivity and response bias. Cue utilization was the only statistically significant adjusted predictor of sensitivity (Table 6: coefficient 0.75, SE 0.21, p<.001; model R²=0.12). Knowledge was not a significant adjusted predictor of sensitivity (p=.263), but was associated with a stronger tendency to classify messages as phishing (Table 7: p=.002) and with more genuine emails misclassified as phishing (Table 9: p=.020). The post-hoc hit-rate analysis also associated greater knowledge with more correct phishing labels (Table 8: p=.015). These are associations, not evidence that changing knowledge or cue utilization causes a behavioral improvement. See §§6.3–6.4, pp. 7–8, Tables 6–9.

**Limitations and audit issue:** The 50% known phishing prevalence, brief fixed viewing time, deliberately planted suspicious features, forced categories and consistent task order make transfer to ordinary work uncertain. The authors explicitly discuss prevalence, time controls and order effects in §7.2, p. 9. There is also a **publication-level reproducibility question**: the printed expression for the decision criterion in §6.2, p. 5 appears inconsistent with the conventional signal-detection definition and with the non-perfect correlation between criterion and sensitivity in Table 5, p. 8. This may be a typesetting error; it is **not evidence** that the underlying analyses used the wrong calculation. Do not independently reproduce criterion estimates from the printed expression without clarification.

## Morrison et al. (2025 issue; online 2024): does a task result transfer?

**Design and population:** Quasi-experimental comparison of measured participant groups, not randomized assignment to cognitive traits. **94** first- and second-year psychology students at Macquarie University, Australia, completed a controlled phishing-judgment task and consented to later simulated messages. The task used **40 email images** (20 genuine, 20 phishing), shown for up to 20 seconds, and recorded classifications and response latency. Participants also completed the CRT-2 and a cue-utilization assessment. See publisher full text, *Method > Participants; Materials > Phishing Decision Task; Procedure; Design and Statistical Analysis*.

**Naturalistic comparison and endpoints:** The researchers planned three later simulated phishing emails; **one was blocked by a spam filter**, leaving two analyzed messages. Outcomes were **not opened**, **opened without link click**, or **clicked link**. Each analyzed message's reported category counts sum to **87**, rather than 94; the full per-message denominator/exclusion path requires further clarification. Opening and clicking do not themselves establish compromise, and a non-open does not prove recognition. See *Naturalistic Phishing Simulation; Results > Cue Utilization, Cognitive Reflection, and Phishing Simulation Engagement*, Table 2.

**Results:** In the controlled task, lower cognitive reflection was associated with more genuine emails incorrectly called phishing (mean 6.50 versus 5.32 out of 20; p=.030; partial eta-squared=.051). Higher cue-utilization grouping was **not** significantly associated with true-positive or false-positive task accuracy in this sample (reported true-positive p=.063). Neither cue utilization nor cognitive reflection significantly predicted the three-category engagement outcome for either of the two simulated inbox messages (cue-utilization p=.293 and p=.533; reflection p=.685 and p=.185). The authors found no statistically significant correlation between the controlled task's components and engagement in the simulated inbox campaign. **A non-significant result is not proof of no effect**, particularly with this sample and only two analyzed messages. See *Results > Phishing Decision Task Accuracy; Phishing Simulation Engagement; Correlations Between Phishing Decision Task and Simulated Phishing Emails*.

**Limitations:** Small, relatively homogeneous student sample; grouping continuous constructs into high/low categories; controlled task with 50:50 phishing prevalence and possible ceiling effects; participants told that phishing emails would arrive; the prior assessments may have primed vigilance; only two delivered/analysed simulated messages. The authors discuss these directly in *Discussion > Limitations*. Their reported field results cannot validate or invalidate an individual-level risk predictor in a different workplace population.

## Older foundation: Canfield et al. (2016), ABSTRACT-ONLY

The publisher abstract describes two scenario-based online experiments that distinguish a **detection judgment** (“is this phishing?”) from a **behavioral judgment** (“what would you do?”). The authors report that cautious stated behavior could coexist with limited detection ability, and that confidence and perceived consequences were associated with response patterns. This is useful conceptual antecedent evidence, but **sample details, statistical estimates and risk of bias were not independently extracted from the full paper here**. The related [2017 replication](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2017/technical-sessions/presentation/canfield) has already been appraised elsewhere in this repository and reported limited predictive validity for its particular external computer-security indicators. Do not pool the two as if they are independent replications of the same outcome.

## What the evidence supports, and what it does not

**Authors' findings:** Sturman et al. observed that higher phishing knowledge tracked both more phishing hits and more mistaken phishing labels for genuine messages, while cue-utilization grouping tracked discrimination. Morrison et al. observed an association between cognitive reflection and fewer controlled-task false positives but did not detect comparable predictors of engagement in their limited inbox simulation. Canfield et al.'s abstract distinguishes detection judgments from stated actions.

**Cross-study interpretation:** A false-positive **classification** and an inappropriate **report** are not the same event. The first is defined against the true label of a message. The second additionally depends on what information was available, whether uncertainty was reasonable, and what the reporting process is for. None of these three papers experimentally determines when a benign-message report was justified at the moment of action. The studies also differ in populations, task format and outcome; their coefficients should not be pooled.

**Proposed future test, not conducted:** In an ethically approved, consented study, present legitimate and suspicious requests of comparable difficulty. Separately observe initial classification, confidence, whether a trusted verification channel is used, whether a report is submitted, and the final action. Predefine both the true message status and what counts as an appropriate action **given the information visible at decision time**. Record legitimate-work disruption and reviewer burden. An increase in reports accompanied by more benign reports might indicate improved help-seeking, a shifted decision threshold or degraded discrimination; it is not automatically one of these.

**Three open questions:** (1) Can decision-time reasonableness be assessed without hindsight from the final ground-truth label? (2) Does the relationship between cue utilization and discrimination persist in ordinary inboxes when people do not expect half of their messages to be malicious? (3) When people are unsure, does access to trusted verification change what they do, rather than only what they say the message is?

**Source-type separation:** All three sources are peer-reviewed journal articles. No news reports, podcasts or preprints are used as empirical evidence in this note.
