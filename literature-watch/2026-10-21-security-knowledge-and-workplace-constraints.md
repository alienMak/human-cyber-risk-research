# Knowing the safer decision is not the same as being able to take it

**Primary paper:** Adam Beautement, Ingolf Becker, Simon Parkin, Kat Krol and Angela Sasse, [Productive Security: A Scalable Methodology for Analysing Employee Security Behaviours](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2016/technical-sessions/presentation/beautement), SOUPS 2016, pp. 253–270.  
**Additional context:** Xiaowei Chen et al., [What Motivates and Discourages Employees in Phishing Interventions](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2024/presentation/chen), SOUPS 2024, pp. 487–506.  
**Evidence status for this note:** Publisher abstracts and bibliographic records checked. ABSTRACT-ONLY synthesis; not a new full-text appraisal.  
**Recorded:** 21 October 2026.

## The distinction I want to test

Knowing what the safer decision is does not necessarily mean someone feels able to make it. Before treating compliance as a failure of awareness, we need to understand what made refusing or verifying the request difficult.

Beautement and colleagues developed a scenario-based survey approach grounded in employee interviews, examining the relationship between organizational security policies, employee goals and capabilities. Their publisher abstract reports differences among groups in a multinational company and describes use of the work to inform policy. It does not establish that any particular unsafe action was caused by an impossible policy.

Chen and colleagues report seven focus groups with 34 university employees. Their publisher abstract identifies feedback, privacy concerns, perceived consequences and the social environment as factors in employees' engagement with phishing interventions and reporting. These are qualitative findings about perceived motivations, not estimates of the causal effect of managerial pressure.

## Competing explanations

If someone follows a suspicious request, at least three explanations should remain open: they did not recognize the risk; they recognized it but lacked a practical way to verify; or they recognized it but believed delay or refusal would carry consequences. Other possibilities include misunderstanding the task or believing the message was legitimate. A single click cannot distinguish these explanations.

**My interpretation, not the papers' combined finding:** The quality of the decision environment is part of what needs to be studied. A security instruction can be understood without being easy to follow in a particular workflow. That is not a blanket excuse for noncompliance; it is a reason to avoid drawing conclusions about awareness from one observed action.

## A test worth designing

In a future consented study, ask participants to judge both legitimate and suspicious requests under differing work demands, and give them realistic opportunities to verify. Record recognition, verification attempts, response choice, perceived costs of delay and mistaken rejection of legitimate work as separate outcomes. Compare behavior when a safe verification route is easy versus difficult to access, without penalizing participants or implying that a simulation measures stable individual risk.

The open question is whether providing a usable verification path changes behavior even when knowledge is held constant. Neither cited abstract answers that question. Full-text extraction of recruitment, scenario construction, subgroup analysis and alternative explanations remains necessary before making stronger claims.
