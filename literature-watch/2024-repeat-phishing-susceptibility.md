# Repeat clicking is a pattern, not an explanation

**Source:** [Not All Victims Are Created Equal: Investigating Differential Phishing Susceptibility](https://www.nist.gov/publications/not-all-victims-are-created-equal-investigating-differential-phishing-susceptibility)  
**Authors:** Matthew Canham, Shaneé Dawkins and Jody Jacobs  
**Published:** September 2024 (NIST record)  
**Source type:** Conference research paper, HCI International 2024  
**Added:** October 2026

## The finding

The study examined repeated phishing clicks in a university context, using participant surveys and eight messages distributed over four weeks: four phishing messages and four controls. Researchers considered individual characteristics, social engineering tactics and contextual factors. Several measured individual differences were associated with repeat clicking; the authors also found that Phish Scale difficulty ratings tracked observed click rates.

## Why this matters for human cyber risk

Repeated risky decisions may be worth tracking, but attaching a repeat-risk label to an individual does not explain why the decisions happened. The difficulty of the message and the setting in which it appeared are part of the measurement.

This is especially relevant when interpreting a longitudinal simulation. If the next round contains harder scenarios, an increase in risky choices might reflect the new challenge rather than a deterioration in the participant's judgment.

## Where the argument needs care

Associations with individual characteristics should not be treated as fixed personal risk profiles. This study also took place shortly after COVID-19 disrupted in-person classes, a context the authors themselves flag as a possible influence.

## Question to pursue

How much of a participant's repeat-risk pattern remains after accounting for scenario difficulty, attack vector and changes in the surrounding context?
