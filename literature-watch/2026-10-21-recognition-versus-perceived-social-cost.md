# Can recognizing a suspicious request still leave someone feeling compelled to act?

**Sources:** [Weidhaas, von Preuschen and Distler (SOUPS 2026)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2026/presentation/weidhaas), pp. 293–312, DOI: https://doi.org/10.60882/cispa.32775066; [Chen et al. (SOUPS 2024)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2024/presentation/chen), pp. 487–506.  
**Source type:** Two peer-reviewed empirical conference studies.  
**Review status for this synthesis:** ABSTRACT-ONLY for both sources in this run; publisher records corroborated. No page-specific full-text methodological claims are made.  
**Recorded:** 21 October 2026. **Prior coverage:** Both papers were already identified in literature-review/evidence-matrix.md or literature-review/full-text-priority.md; this note develops a different question, not a new bibliographic discovery.

## What the authors reported

Weidhaas and colleagues describe a qualitative simulated-workplace study with 41 participants, combining observation, interviews and questionnaires. Their abstract reports that not clicking a phishing link could be appraised as a possible mistake with reputational consequences. It does not establish that participants knowingly complied with an identified phishing attempt, or measure a causal effect of authority.

Chen and colleagues describe seven focus groups with 34 employees at a European university. Their abstract reports that the perceived value of interventions, fear of consequences, feedback, privacy concerns, colleagues and supervisors shaped engagement with phishing training and reporting. This is qualitative evidence about reported motivations, not a randomized test of workplace authority or observed compliance.

## Where the studies meet, and where they do not

**Authors' findings:** One study reports perceived reputational costs around refusing a suspicious request. The other reports social and organizational factors shaping security-intervention engagement and reporting.

**Analytical interpretation:** An employee might experience two competing risks: the technical risk of following a suspicious request and the social or practical risk of declining something that appears legitimate. This is a plausible explanation to test, not an established combined finding. Recognition, perceived consequences, compliance, verification and reporting must remain distinct.

**Proposed future test, not conducted:** In an ethically approved, consented study, compare responses to legitimate and suspicious requests while independently varying apparent authority and urgency. Record perceived legitimacy, stated concern, verification, compliance and rejection of legitimate requests as separate outcomes. Use debriefing and avoid employment consequences. The crucial subgroup would be people who identify suspicious cues but still act; determine whether their explanation involves authority, workload, uncertainty, or something else. Do not infer an individual trait from a single scenario.

## Limits and follow-up reading

Neither paper's full text was appraised in this run. Before claiming frequencies, participant quotations, experimental contrasts or detailed causal pathways, read the original methods and results, especially recruitment, simulation design, operationalization of emotions or consequences, and negative cases. A separate 2019 journal abstract on workplace spear phishing reports an association between authority cues and clicks in organizational simulations, but its full methods were not verified here and it is not used to establish the mechanism.
