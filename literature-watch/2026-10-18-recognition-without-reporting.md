# Recognizing a suspicious email is not the same as reporting it

**Source:** [Chen, Doublet, Sergeeva, Lenzini, Koenig and Distler, *What Motivates and Discourages Employees in Phishing Interventions: An Exploration of Expectancy-Value Theory*](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/soups2024-chen.pdf), SOUPS 2024, pp. 487–506.  
**Type:** Peer-reviewed primary qualitative research. **Access:** FULL TEXT, sections 1–4 and methods/limitations reviewed; not a quantitative intervention appraisal. **Date:** 18 October 2026.

## What the authors investigated

The study used **seven focus groups with 34 employees** of a research-oriented European university, including **19 researchers, 12 administrative staff and 3 software developers**. Forty-five people registered interest; 34 attended between November 2022 and January 2023 (§3.2, p. 490). Each focus group had three to seven participants and lasted about 90 minutes. The researchers used Expectancy-Value Theory to examine why employees engaged with or avoided simulated phishing tests, training and phishing reporting (§§2.3–3.3, pp. 489–490).

The university ran monthly simulated phishing tests, offered online security courses and encouraged employees to report suspicious email. An automatic response acknowledged reported simulations; manual review of other reports normally took one or two working days (§3.1, pp. 489–490). **This is contextual information about the studied organization, not evidence that its procedures were optimal.**

Audio from the groups was transcribed and coded using an iterative thematic process. The authors explicitly state that themes were co-created in group discussions and that counting how often a theme was mentioned would not be meaningful (§§3.4, 4, pp. 490–491). Ethics approval, consent, pseudonymization and data handling are described in §3.5.

## What the paper supports

Participants described both motivations to engage and barriers to reporting. Perceived usefulness, expected benefit, fear of consequences, social roles, colleagues and supervisors shaped discussion of engagement; lack of feedback, worries and privacy concerns could discourage reporting (Abstract; §4). These are **reported perceptions and themes**, not observed individual causal effects.

The paper also discusses participants' perception that training can be less useful under stress or time pressure (§4.1.1, p. 491). This does **not** experimentally demonstrate that stress caused a particular phishing failure.

## Twin's competing explanations

If an employee does not report a suspicious message, plausible explanations include:
1. They did not recognize the message as suspicious.
2. They recognized it but did not know the reporting route or expected little benefit.
3. They worried about privacy, consequences or burdening colleagues.
4. They ignored the message and did not see reporting as necessary.

**The absence of a report alone does not discriminate among these explanations.** Conversely, a report does not automatically prove that the person accurately identified an attack; legitimate messages can be reported as suspicious.

## What this changes

The prior notes distinguish recognition, verification, final action and retrospective explanation. This paper adds **organizational conditions surrounding reporting**. A general future study could separately examine whether participants recognize suspicious messages, know how to report, perceive reporting as safe and useful, and actually report. These are candidate research questions, not a validated instrument or proprietary scoring method.

**Not established:** that the identified barriers cause non-reporting in a representative workforce; that more reporting always means better detection; or that a particular intervention would remove the barriers.

**Researcher review question:** When someone notices a suspicious message but does not report it, are we measuring their security judgment or the reporting environment they operate within?

**Public boundary:** Published research and general methods only; no private algorithms, platform architecture, participant data or confidential organizational information.
