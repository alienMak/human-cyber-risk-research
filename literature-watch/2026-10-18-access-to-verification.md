# Recognizing risk is not the same as having a way to check it

**Source:** [Buckmann, Nold, Acar and Zou, *More than Usability: Differential Access to Digital Security and Privacy*](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/soups2025-buckmann.pdf)  
**Published:** SOUPS 2025, pp. 455–474. **Added:** 18 October 2026.  
**Type:** Peer-reviewed qualitative research. **Access:** FULL TEXT APPRAISED for the questions below.

## What the authors investigated

The authors used 47 semi-structured interviews with people of varied backgrounds in Germany to examine security/privacy awareness, motivation and ability using the Security and Privacy Acceptance Framework (SPAF). The study was not a phishing intervention or a prospective test of scam outcomes. Participants were recruited through organizations and snowball sampling; interviews were remote, in German, and analyzed thematically (§3.1, pp. 457–458). Two researchers independently coded 14 interviews; one coded the rest, with continuing discussion and review. The authors did not report inter-rater reliability (§3.1).

The authors identify uneven access to knowledgeable support, understandable resources and the means to act on security advice. Participants described relying on relatives, institutional assistance or online searching; knowledgeable enthusiasts had more established information sources than many everyday users (§4.2, pp. 458–459). The paper calls this broader pattern **differential access**. This is the authors' construct, not a novel idea originating in this repository.

## What the comparison changes

Earlier notes asked whether someone who keeps a suspicious caller on the line while searching for confirmation is cautious or still exposed to manipulation. The Buckmann paper adds a third possibility: the person may lack an accessible, trustworthy alternative source of help.

**Authors' finding:** Access to security knowledge and support differs across participants' circumstances (§§4.2–4.5).  
**Analysis:** A verification attempt is not automatically independent just because someone searches online. Nor does continued engagement necessarily mean the person failed to recognize the risk. The study documents reported access barriers; it does **not** observe callers remaining on a live scam or establish that a particular barrier caused compliance.

## Critical appraisal

| Domain | Assessment | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design | Partial | Interviews are appropriate for exploring barriers, not causal effects (§3.1). |
| Sampling | Partial | Diverse purposive/organizational and snowball recruitment; not population-representative (§§3.1–3.2). |
| Outcomes | Partial | Accounts of practices and support; no observed phishing decisions (§§3.1, 4). |
| Confounding | Partial | Intersecting social circumstances considered qualitatively; no randomized comparison (§§3.1, 4). |
| Measurement | Partial | Interview guide and thematic coding described; self-report and recall limitations (§§3.1–3.2). |
| Analysis | Partial | Dual coding for 14 interviews and consensus review; no numerical inter-rater agreement (§3.1). |
| Generalization | Partial | Germany-only, remote interviews, nonrepresentative sample (§3.2). |
| Ethics/reproducibility | Partial | Consent, privacy procedures and data protection officer approval described; institution lacked an ethics review board (§3.2). |

## Three research questions, not established effects

1. Does an accessible independent verification route change a decision after initial suspicion, compared with continued contact with the requester?
2. Can apparent differences in security behavior be partly explained by access to trustworthy assistance rather than recognition alone?
3. When participants describe a past security decision, can prospective or contemporaneous evidence distinguish the original reasoning from later reconstruction?

The third question is **not tested** by Buckmann et al.; the authors themselves acknowledge recall and social-desirability limitations (§3.2, p. 457). See also [Abels, Smith and Ortloff (SOUPS 2025)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2025/presentation/abels), **publisher abstract checked here only**, for a separate instrument addressing socially desirable security/privacy responses. That instrument cannot establish whether a specific retrospective explanation is false.

**Open question for researcher review:** Are we evaluating whether people know they should verify, or whether they have a genuinely independent way to do so when the decision arrives?
