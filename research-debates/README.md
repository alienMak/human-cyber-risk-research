# Where the evidence does not give one simple answer

**Prepared 13 October 2026.** This is a disagreement and boundary-condition register, not a vote-counting exercise. Publisher abstracts and existing repository records were consulted; no new full-text appraisal is claimed.

## Debate 1: Does phishing training produce lasting behavior change?

[Reinheimer et al. (SOUPS 2020)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2020/presentation/reinheimer) studied 409 public-administration employees and reported improved classification of phishing and legitimate emails immediately and at four months, but not at six months; video and interactive reminders performed well in the studied setting.

The repository's [evidence matrix](../literature-review/evidence-matrix.md) also includes the 2025 IEEE S&P field experiment [Understanding the Efficacy of Phishing Training in Practice](https://doi.org/10.1109/SP61157.2025.00076), described there as finding limited practical benefit for conventional training on simulated-phishing outcomes.

**Not necessarily a contradiction:** classification in a study, simulated click behavior, reporting, follow-up time and organizational context are not interchangeable outcomes. A fair comparison needs intervention assignment, baseline, lure difficulty, timing and uncertainty from the original full texts.

## Debate 2: Does giving users more information make verification better?

[Zheng & Becker (SOUPS 2022)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2022/presentation/zheng) found that showing user-facing email headers alone did not improve phishing detection in their experiment. [Zheng & Becker (SOUPS 2023)](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2023/presentation/zheng) found that professional email users in a 27-person formative evaluation considered a check button and suspicion-score nudge useful.

**Not necessarily a contradiction:** passive visibility of cues and a designed verification action are different interventions. Perceived usefulness is not the same as measured reduction in harmful actions.

## Debate 3: Are click rates evidence of individual vulnerability?

[Steves, Greene & Theofanos (2020)](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/journal/2020/09/categorizing-human-phishing-detection-difficulty-a/final) argue that email cues and premise alignment can affect difficulty, making unadjusted click rates hard to interpret across exercises.

**Open question:** how much observed variation belongs to the person, the task, the environment or their interaction? The initial Phish Scale evidence does not validate a stable person-level trait or extend automatically to telephone and physical scenarios.
