# When trust is justified, not merely manufactured

A history of phishing and impersonation can leave the impression that trusting technology is inherently mistaken. That would be too easy a conclusion. People depend on systems, institutions and each other; the research question is when reliance is justified and how they can tell.

## Three different situations

**Authentic and verifiable.** A claim can be checked against a source with a genuinely independent basis, such as a known contact channel or authenticated record. The important issue is not how official the interface looks, but what the verification actually establishes.

**Authentic but hard to verify.** Legitimate communication can be unfamiliar, urgent or poorly designed. A defensive rule that treats every unusual request as malicious can create friction and missed opportunities. Usable verification is therefore also a legitimate-service design problem.

**Convincing but unverified.** Branding, fluent language and apparent confidence can make a false claim seem credible. None is independent evidence of truth or identity.

The [2022 SOUPS study on email headers](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2022/presentation/zheng) provides a caution about merely showing more sender details. The [2023 SOUPS usability study](https://www.usenix.org/conference/soups2023/presentation/zheng) asks how checking tools might fit users' work. Neither proves a universally best verification interface.

## Questions for research

What does a legitimate institution owe a recipient who is asked to act? How can systems provide trustworthy routes to confirmation without assuming users have unlimited time or technical knowledge? How can an AI tool express uncertainty and trace its evidence without creating a false impression that a citation is already verified?

These are public research questions, not findings from a new experiment.
