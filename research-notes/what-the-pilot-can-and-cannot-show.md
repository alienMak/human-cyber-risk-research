# What the early pilot can and cannot show

The VISHWAR capstone study involved 20 participants and explored security decisions after a sequence that included static training, a standardized phishing exercise, and VISHWAR scenarios.

What interested me was not just whether someone selected a safe option, but whether their responses changed when the context and attack vector changed.

There is an important limitation here. A click in a standardized phishing exercise and a risky decision in a multi-vector simulation are not identical outcomes. Comparing their percentages as though they measure the same behavior would make the study sound more conclusive than it is.

The pilot was exploratory. It offered questions worth following up, not proof that one training approach caused better behavior.

## Questions worth revisiting

- How consistently does a participant respond across phishing, impersonation, and other simulated pressure?
- Is an apparent improvement specific to the scenario, or does it transfer?
- What do response delays mean when different decisions have different levels of complexity?
- How should uncertainty appear alongside any summary risk score?
- What would a stronger comparative design need to control?

## What a follow-up study would need

Clear outcome definitions, comparable conditions, a documented sampling plan, and an analysis that separates knowledge recall from behavior under pressure. I'd also want to be careful about treating an exploratory sample as representative of a larger population.

This is a discussion of study design and interpretation, not a release of participant-level records or a claim of validated effectiveness.
