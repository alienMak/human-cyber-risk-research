# QT-004 — When does credibility get mistaken for proof?

**Recorded in this repository:** 18 October 2026  
**Earlier documented context:** 13 October 2026 — Related public verification and credibility questions were documented in the research-question register dated 13 October 2026; this is not the first proven discussion date.  
**Origin:** Reconstructed from the repository's existing trust and verification research, including publisher-sourced email studies; conceptual questions are not attributed as verbatim user quotes.  
**Status:** Open research trail; reconstructed conceptual analysis, not a verbatim transcript.

## Brief

A familiar name, polished message or plausible context can make a request believable without proving its origin. The challenge is to tell whether the person checked an independent fact or merely found another cue consistent with the story.

## Question chain

| Step | Question | Working answer | Next question or implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Q1 | What makes a request seem credible? | Context, familiarity, authority cues and presentation may contribute, but the relative effects vary by task. | Does credibility establish authenticity? |
| Q2 | Does a matching sender detail prove identity? | No. An apparently matching detail can still be manipulated or misinterpreted. | What evidence is independent of the requester? |
| Q3 | Can a checking interface create false confidence? | It could, particularly when the tool's limitations are misunderstood. | Does the user understand what was checked? |
| Q4 | Does more visible information always improve detection? | No; the existing email-header experiment challenges that assumption in its setting. | Which information actually changes decisions? |
| Q5 | What would test whether independence matters? | Compare attacker-provided cues with independently obtained confirmation while matching task difficulty and effort. | Can we observe actual verification rather than reported intent? |

## Competing explanations

1. Additional cues improve discrimination.
2. Additional cues raise confidence without improving discrimination.
3. An accessible independent route improves decisions but its friction limits use.

## Provisional position and confidence

Credibility is not proof of origin. High confidence in this logical distinction; the impact of particular interface interventions remains context-dependent.

## Next test

Develop an ethically appropriate, pre-specified comparison with aligned observable outcomes, legitimate control cases where applicable, scenario-context documentation, and a way to discriminate the competing explanations. Record what would change the conclusion before interpreting results.
