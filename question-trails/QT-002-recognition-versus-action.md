# QT-002 — Recognizing a threat is not the same as acting safely

**Recorded in this repository:** 18 October 2026  
**Earlier documented context:** 6 August 2026 — Related recognition-versus-action research framing was discussed on 6 August 2026; this is not a verified first occurrence of the question.  
**Origin:** Reconstructed from the existing public research agenda on recognition, confidence and behavior under pressure; not a verbatim conversation.  
**Status:** Open research trail; reconstructed conceptual analysis, not a verbatim transcript.

## Brief

A person can recognize a suspicious request and still comply. Conversely, someone may reject a legitimate request without having recognized any threat. A click/no-click result alone does not tell us which process occurred.

## Question chain

| Step | Question | Working answer | Next question or implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Q1 | Can a non-click prove the person recognized phishing? | No. It might reflect suspicion, disinterest, avoidance or an unrelated interruption. | How do we separate recognition from behavior? |
| Q2 | Can someone recognize a threat and still comply? | Yes as a plausible behavioral pattern, but its frequency and causes require direct observation. | What prevents acting on recognition? |
| Q3 | Could a verification option change behavior without changing initial recognition? | Plausibly. Accessible verification could alter action after uncertainty, but that is a testable hypothesis. | How do we distinguish available options from actually used options? |
| Q4 | Could lower click rates conceal indiscriminate rejection? | Yes. Include legitimate requests and track false alarms. | What is a fair outcome measure? |
| Q5 | What would challenge the recognition-action distinction? | Well-aligned observational data showing recognition and action almost always coincide across settings would weaken its practical importance. | Does the relationship generalize beyond email? |

## Competing explanations

1. Knowledge deficit: users misidentify the request.
2. Action barrier: users suspect danger but lack a usable verification or refusal path.
3. Measurement artifact: a non-click is misclassified as an informed safe decision.
