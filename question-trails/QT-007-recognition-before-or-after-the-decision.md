# QT-007 — Did they recognize the threat before deciding, or only afterward?

**Earlier documented context:** 17 October 2026 — A Twin research discussion separated prior recognition from hindsight reconstruction and socially desirable explanations. The chain below is a public reconstruction, not a transcript of the conversation.
**Origin:** Follow-up to the recognition-versus-action discussion in [QT-002](QT-002-recognition-versus-action.md) and the [recognition-to-action research agenda](../literature-review/recognition-to-action-research-questions.md).
**Status:** Open. The interpretation is conceptual, not a new empirical result.

## Brief

If someone clicks and then says they knew the request looked suspicious, the statement may be true. It may also be influenced by knowing the outcome, by embarrassment, or by the language of the debrief. Treating the explanation as a direct window into what they knew before deciding skips an important measurement problem.

## Question chain

| Step | Question | Working answer | Why it leads further |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Q1 | If someone says afterward, “I knew it looked suspicious,” does that prove they recognized it before acting? | No. Their account is evidence of a later report, not automatically evidence of their earlier mental state. | What would establish the timing of recognition? |
| Q2 | Would asking them immediately after the choice solve the problem? | It would reduce delay but not remove reconstruction, justification or the influence of feedback. | Could we capture their assessment before they see the outcome? |
| Q3 | Would a pre-decision suspicion prompt give us the answer? | It would supply a time-anchored observation, but the prompt itself might make suspicion more salient and change the decision. | How can measurement avoid creating the behavior it claims to observe? |
| Q4 | Could observable hesitation or checking substitute for asking? | Such behavior may indicate uncertainty, but can also reflect distraction, interface friction or simple task difficulty. No single proxy establishes recognition. | Would several independent observations improve interpretation? |
| Q5 | What if the person truly recognized the threat but complied anyway? | Then recognition and action diverged. We need to keep that possibility separate from a person who only reports recognition after the fact. | Which explanations can the available observations actually rule out? |
| Q6 | What if someone safely refused but cannot explain why? | Safe behavior alone does not establish accurate recognition; they may have avoided all unfamiliar requests. | Do both suspicious and legitimate examples matter? |
| Q7 | What conclusion is defensible without time-anchored evidence? | Only that the person reported earlier recognition retrospectively. A stronger claim would need observations supporting when they recognized the threat and what they did next. | The causal interpretation remains open. |

## Reasoning in more detail

Consider a hypothetical participant who approves an unexpected request. After being told it was suspicious, they explain that the unusual wording had bothered them from the beginning. That might be an accurate recollection of a concern they set aside under pressure. Or it might be a plausible narrative assembled after feedback. Those interpretations imply different problems: knowing but complying versus failing to recognize until afterward.

Now consider another participant who pauses, checks the sender name, and then approves the request. The pause looks useful, but it does not tell us whether they identified the actual inconsistency. Recording more signals is not automatically the same as recording a more valid construct.

The important distinction is between **the time of the decision**, **the time of the explanation**, and **the time at which evidence about recognition becomes available**. Those are often collapsed into one story. This trail does not assume there is a neutral way to read an internal state directly from a click, a pause or a retrospective answer.

## Competing explanations

- **Recognition followed by compliance:** the person noticed the threat cue but prioritized another perceived obligation.
- **Recognition formed after feedback:** the outcome changed how the earlier scene was remembered or described.
- **Impression management:** the person describes themselves as more cautious than their contemporaneous behavior supports.
- **Unmeasured uncertainty:** behavior reflected confusion or difficulty unrelated to recognizing an attack.

These explanations may overlap. Without appropriately timed observations, choosing one from the participant's final explanation would overstate the evidence.

## Provisional position and confidence

**High confidence in the logical distinction:** a retrospective explanation and a contemporaneous observation are not interchangeable. **Low confidence in any specific prevalence or causal attribution:** the linked material does not establish how often people reconstruct prior recognition after feedback. The honest conclusion is to preserve those competing explanations instead of labeling later self-report as proof of earlier awareness.
